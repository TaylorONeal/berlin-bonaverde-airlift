# The Berlin story

For anyone who finds a Berlin in a cupboard and wonders what it was. Facts carry a source at the bottom. Opinions are marked as opinions. Corrections are welcome: open an issue with a source and we'll fix it and credit you.

## A genuinely great idea

Coffee leaves the farm green. It keeps well that way, and it starts going stale once it's roasted. Bonaverde's idea was to close that gap: a countertop machine that takes green beans and roasts, grinds and brews them in one run, minutes before the water hits. Plenty of machines grind and brew. Almost none roast too.

Green coffee also points straight back at the people who grow it. We love that part. A machine that runs on green beans is a machine that can run on beans from a small co-op in Guatemala, or anywhere else a grower is doing careful work.

## The campaign and the machine

Bonaverde ran a Kickstarter in 2013 and raised more than $680,000 from more than 2,200 backers. [1][2] The early design that backers saw was a curved wooden machine, closer to a piece of mid-century furniture than a kitchen appliance. The Berlin that shipped was a white box. [1][2]

The Berlin reads an NFC card before every run. The card tells it what to do: roast, grind, brew, how hot, how long. Cards came on Bonaverde's bean pouches, and universal cards existed for other beans. [1]

## The end of the company

Bonaverde filed for insolvency in October 2019. [3] Its founder said at the time that about 5,000 of the 20,000 pre-financed machines had been delivered, and that they work flawlessly. [4]

## The lesson (opinion)

This is a textbook product and project management story, and we tell it with affection, because the machine is good.

- **A machine that needs one company's cards lasts as long as that company.** The hardware outlived the business. The card supply was the weak link, not the roaster.
- **Lock-in is a bet the owner pays for later.** Tying every brew to a card from one source can look like a business model at launch. It turns into the owner's problem the day the source stops.
- **Design promises are product promises.** The curved wood was part of why people backed it. Shipping something different is a decision worth explaining well.
- **Open formats are a gift to your future customers.** If the card format had been published, owners would never have had to work it out.

## The owners fixed it

What happened next belongs to the community. Owners pooled cards and read them. PostalBlab's hackaverde project published the card layout and real card dumps. Craig Whyte, Michael Grundler and others shared profiles. Bernd Trappe's VerdeNFC turned it all into an app that writes working, reusable cards. Marcus Wolschon's raspiverde put a Raspberry Pi in charge. Nobody was paid. They just decided a good machine they owned should keep working.

## This app

Berlin Airlift (2026) is a deliberately simple front end on that work. One screen, one big button, a read-back check on every card, and a design that gives the Berlin back a little of the curved wood it was first drawn with. It's free and open source, so it can't be locked away either.
